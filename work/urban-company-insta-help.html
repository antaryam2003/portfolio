Insta Help: one price, one source of truth

Urban Company's InstaHelp won the instant-house-help category in eleven months — 51,520 bookings in a single day, over a million in a month, five metros. It also loses ₹447 on every order.

So I didn't write a growth PRD. I wrote one for three defects that customers experience as dishonesty, partners experience as an algorithm they can't see, and finance experiences as paying the burn twice for one satisfied customer. All three are the same class of bug: two systems telling two people different versions of the truth.

01 · The product

Quick commerce, pointed at household labour

InstaHelp sits inside the main Urban Company app as a vertical beside Beauty, Repairs and Native. You book cleaning, dishwashing, laundry or meal prep and a trained, background-verified professional arrives in 10–15 minutes — priced and paid for like a grocery order, not a home-service appointment.

Leadership has called it the template for making the company's whole catalogue instantaneous. It is also the single biggest drag on its P&L, and the two facts are the same fact: growth is bought with price.

At −₹447 an order, a booking that fails and has to be re-placed burns roughly ₹894 to deliver one satisfied customer, plus the refund and the support contact. InstaHelp is the rare product where a trust defect and a unit-economics defect are the same line item.

02 · The problem

The promise lands. The paperwork around it doesn't.

Nobody complains that the professional didn't arrive in fifteen minutes. The core promise is landing — that's what the growth curve says. What people complain about is everything the app tells them around the arrival: the price, who's free, and whether the booking ever existed.

I read customer reviews across Trustpilot, MouthShut and PissedConsumer alongside union statements, labour reporting and the company's own filings, and sorted every recurring complaint by who it hits and what it actually is. Six moments, two sides of the door.

03 · The reframe

Three complaints. One bug. Two apps that disagree about reality.

The reframe

Insta Help's trust problem is a consistency problem wearing three costumes. The price the customer is quoted, the availability the customer is shown, and the booking the customer can find later are all cases of one system knowing something a second system never says out loud.

That changed what I was writing. “Improve trust” is a brand programme with no owner and no target. “Make every surface read the same source” is an engineering requirement with a number attached — and the number should be zero.

Three answers to one question
“Is this professional free right now?” — asked of three surfaces, today
Customer app
“Unavailable”
No reason, no time, no alternative with a why.
Support script
“Slots are booked”
An answer the agent cannot verify and cannot override.
Partner app
“Idle, waiting”
Logged in, unpaid, and being told nothing is available.
Proposed
One availability service.
Three surfaces read it.
01The customer sees real status with a real reason — “on another job until 4:40pm”, never a bare “unavailable”.
02The agent reads the same record the customer does, so the script stops contradicting the professional.
03Ops plan against available supply, not logged-in supply — which is also the fix for the attendance regime.
This is the document's one architectural ask, and it pays for itself three times: it closes the pack-redemption complaint, gives ops a supply metric worth planning on, and removes the reason to penalise attendance. Everything in Release 2 depends on it.

05 · The proposal

Make the app say out loud what the system already knows

In one line: one honest price before booking, one real answer about who's free, one place every booking can be found — and a supply side that can verify the promise made to it.

01
The price you see is the price you pay

All-in at the moment of service selection. Any surcharge for a preferred professional or slot is priced before that choice is confirmed, never at checkout.

02
Failure resolves without a human

No OTP, no-show, disputed job: every booking is logged, verifiable failures settle themselves against GPS and timestamps, and refund state is always visible.

03
Partners can audit their own deal

Rules stated in plain terms, standing visible, logged-in hours plotted against jobs received, safety owned en route, and deactivation appealable to a person.

Journeys A and B hold five of the eight P0s between them — the two journeys where the customer is deciding whether to trust a number. That weighting is the argument: at −₹447 an order, the cheapest thing to fix is the sentence the app says before anyone commits.
Journey A
Booking, and the first price you see
P0The price shown at service selection is the final, all-in price for the base service. No delta appears later in the flow.
P0The start-of-job OTP appears in at least two places — push and a persistent live-order banner — and is resendable in-app without contacting support.

P2First-time users get one screen on what is verified about their professional: background check, training, insurance.
Journey B
Choosing a specific professional or slot
P0If a preference changes the price, the delta is shown before the choice is confirmed — never revealed at checkout.
P0“Unavailable” is read from the same availability record the partner app shows. One source of truth, three surfaces.
P1If she's genuinely busy, offer the next-best match with the reason attached — “on another job until 4:40pm”.
Journey C
Redeeming a prepaid Insta Pack
P0Packs reach the same professional-rating pool as pay-as-you-go bookings. Any exception is disclosed at purchase, not discovered at redemption.
P1Balance, expiry and usage history on one screen, with a nudge before hours expire.
Journey D
When something goes wrong
P0Every booking — successful or failed — appears in order history with a status and, where relevant, a refund or dispute state.
P0Verifiable failures — GPS/timestamp mismatch, OTP never delivered — refund self-serve, with no live agent required.
P1Safety complaints (theft, misconduct) route to a dedicated trust & safety queue with a committed SLA — not the general support script.
Journey E · supply side
The professional's side of the same loop
P0Any penalty tied to logging out, and any deprioritisation tied to refusals, is stated in plain terms in the partner app: what triggers it, how much, and current standing.
P1Either a minimum-earnings guarantee, or her own logged-in-hours-versus-jobs-received history, shown before she commits to a long stretch. The flexibility claim becomes checkable.
P1A safety check-in en route: shareable live location with a trusted contact and a one-tap alert — answering directly the reported practice of bringing a male relative for want of a platform net.
P2A transparent appeal for deactivation or rating-driven deprioritisation, with a human review step before anything permanent.
Journey F · supply side
City ops and the 15-minute promise
P1A live view of demand against available — not merely logged-in — professionals per micro-market, so surge pay targets real density gaps instead of blanket attendance rules.
P2Alerting when a zone structurally misses its ETA, so supply gets invested in rather than the customer-facing promise quietly drifting.
This is the journey that makes the supply-side asks affordable. You cannot stop penalising attendance until you can measure readiness — and you cannot promise fifteen minutes on a number that counts people who aren't actually free.

09 · Scope discipline

What this document refuses to touch

The temptation with a loss-making vertical is to write the pricing memo. I didn't, deliberately: the rate card is a finance call, and a PRD that reaches for it loses the right to be judged on anything. Each exclusion below is stated as a constraint the requirements have to live inside.

Out of scope
The rate card and unit economics

The ₹150–180/hour pay structure and the customer price level stay put. This document is about the consistency of what's charged and matched, not the level.

Out of scope
Employment classification

Employee versus contractor is live in courts and legislatures. Not a product decision — but every supply-side requirement here holds either way.

Out of scope
New cities and categories

Nothing beyond the five live metros. Where expansion intersects — pacing a launch against available supply — it appears as a constraint in journey F, not a plan.

Out of scope
Other Urban Company verticals

Beauty, Repairs and Native are out — except where shared matching, payments or support infrastructure is the thing being fixed, which is most of R2.

10 · Open questions

The four I couldn't answer from outside

These are in the document because pretending to know them would have been the least honest thing available. Each one is named with what it blocks, so nobody has to guess which requirement is standing on air.

Question or assumption What the answer changes Blocks
AssumptionCustomer-facing and partner-facing availability come from different systems or cadences, and can be unified without rebuilding the matching engine. If it's one system already, then “unavailable” is a deliberate matching policy and this becomes a disclosure requirement, not an engineering one. Cheaper — and much harder to defend publicly. Needs engineering validation before R2 is sized. R2 entirely
Open questionIs the pack-redemption quality gap a policy, an artefact of how packs are matched, or a perception problem? Whether journey C is a policy change, a bug fix, or a communication fix. One data query — average rating of professionals served to pack holders versus pay-as-you-go — settles it. The first number I'd ask for. Journey C
Open questionWhat minimum-earnings floor keeps the 15-minute promise commercially viable? Whether the fix for “eleven hours, no jobs, no pay” is a guarantee or only radical transparency about the odds. A cross-functional call between product, ops and finance — and at −₹447 an order, finance has the loudest vote. Journey E, P1
Open questionShould the safety check-in be default-on or opt-in? Default-on protects more people; opt-in respects autonomy on a workforce already tracked more than it would like. I lean default-on with a visible off switch, but this is a decision to make with partners and their unions, not for them. Journey E, P1

12 · What writing this taught me

Five things I'd carry into the next document

01
Sort complaints by root cause, not by symptom

Six unrelated-looking grievances collapsed into three defect classes the moment I stopped grouping them by who was angry. The classification was the analysis — everything after it was bookkeeping.

02
On a loss-making vertical, tie trust to the P&L or nobody funds it

“Customers feel misled” competes with growth for budget. “Every avoidable re-booking burns ₹447 twice” doesn't compete with growth — it is growth. Same requirements, completely different meeting.

03
A proxy metric becomes a policy, and then a grievance

Nobody set out to build an eleven-hour attendance regime. Someone needed a supply number, “logged in” was the one available, and the penalty followed the metric. Fixing the metric was the humane move and the operational one.

04
Two-sided products need two-sided requirements

Two of six journeys never touch a customer, and they carry the requirement I'd defend hardest. A trust document that only protects the buyer isn't a trust document — and in this category, the supply side is the thing unions, regulators and press are all watching.

05
What I'd do differently

My first draft treated the pack-redemption complaint as a proven policy failure, and wrote a policy fix for it. That was the least defensible page in the document: reviews prove a pattern is felt, never that it's systematic. Rewriting it as audit first, then policy cost me the most dramatic recommendation in the PRD and made the rest of it credible. If I'd had one internal query, I'd have spent it there.
