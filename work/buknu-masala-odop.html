Design-led market expansion for Buknu Masala, an ODOP product

Buknu is a traditional spice blend from Kanpur Dehat, made by women’s self-help groups and recognised under Uttar Pradesh’s One District One Product scheme. The recipe has survived generations. The business around it had not been designed at all.

Role
Product & strategy lead
Course
DES627 — Management of Design & Innovation, IIT Kanpur
Timeline
Jan – April 2026
Focus
Value chain, brand, packaging, dual-channel GTM

The context

One District One Product exists to turn regional specialities into livelihoods. The theory is elegant: every district has something it makes better than anywhere else, so give that product institutional support and the local economy grows around it.

In practice, the products that struggle rarely struggle because of the product. Buknu sells well within its own district and barely exists outside it — not because people elsewhere wouldn’t like it, but because everything around the recipe was built for a market where the buyer already knows the maker.

Diagnosis: where the value chain broke

I mapped the chain end to end — sourcing, production, packing, distribution, sale — and looked for the points where value was being created and then quietly lost. Four gaps came out of it:

  • Branding. The product had a name and no identity. Nothing told a stranger what it was, what to do with it, or why this one rather than the mass-market jar beside it.
  • Packaging. Functional for a local sale, unfit for a shelf — no durability for transit, no compliance-ready labelling, no shelf presence, no format that suits a household that has never cooked with it.
  • Standardization. Batch-to-batch variation is charming when you buy from a neighbour and disqualifying when you sell to a retailer or a manufacturer. It also makes pricing, shelf-life claims and repeat purchase impossible to reason about.
  • Market access. No route to a buyer more than a district away, and no commercial relationship that could absorb volume if one appeared.

The insight

Locally, trust is carried by the person selling it to you. Three hundred kilometres away, the packaging has to do that job — and consistency is the only way it can. Standardization wasn’t a manufacturing detail; it was the precondition for every channel we wanted.

The growth strategy

I designed the response as three linked moves rather than a list of improvements, because each one only works if the others land:

  1. Brand identity. Give the product a story that’s true and specific — a Kanpur Dehat original, made by the people who have always made it. Provenance is the one thing a national FMCG competitor structurally cannot copy, so the identity was built on it rather than on generic “authentic & traditional” language.
  2. Retail-ready packaging. Designed for the realities of a shelf: survives transit, carries the mandatory information, communicates use-case at a glance to someone who has never tasted Buknu, and offers a small trial format alongside the regular pack so first purchase isn’t a leap of faith.
  3. Product standardization. Documented ratios, defined process steps and repeatable quality checks — simple enough to be run by the SHGs themselves, strict enough that a food manufacturer can specify it in a purchase order.

Why two channels, not one

The obvious plan is to build a consumer brand. It’s also the slow, expensive plan — brand equity takes years and marketing budget the producers don’t have. So I built a dual-channel expansion model where each channel pays for the other’s weakness:

  • B2C retail carries the margin and builds the brand. Slower to scale, but it’s what makes the product an asset rather than a commodity, and it’s where the provenance story earns a price premium.
  • B2B to food manufacturers carries the volume. Selling Buknu as a specified ingredient means predictable orders, no shelf competition, and no need for consumer brand recognition — revenue that stabilises SHG income while the retail brand is still being built.

The two channels have opposite risk profiles, which is the point. B2B is concentrated — lose a buyer, lose a lot — while B2C is fragmented and slow. Run together, they hedge each other. And both are unlocked by the same investment: standardization. That was the argument for sequencing it first.

Designing within the constraint that mattered

The hard constraint wasn’t cost or timeline — it was that production is SHG-led, and that is the point of the scheme, not an inconvenience in the way of it. A strategy that solved the market problem by centralising production into a factory would have scored well on paper and defeated the entire purpose of ODOP.

So every recommendation had to pass three tests:

  • Can the SHGs actually run it? Low capital outlay, learnable in a training session, no dependence on specialist equipment.
  • Does the value stay local? Growth that gets captured by an intermediary is not growth for the district.
  • Does it scale without breaking either of the above? If demand tripled, does the model hold or collapse?

This is where design thinking stopped being a course requirement and started being useful. Empathising with the producer — not just the consumer — is what kept the strategy implementable.

Outcome

The project delivered a full expansion strategy: a value-chain diagnosis with the four gaps named and prioritised, a brand identity and retail-ready packaging direction, a standardization protocol scoped for SHG-scale production, and a dual-channel model with a clear sequencing argument for why standardization comes first.

Framed against ODOP’s own goals, the strategy connects SHG-led production to scalable market opportunity without asking the producers to stop being the producers.

What I took from it

  • The bottleneck is usually one step upstream of where it hurts. The visible problem was market access. The actual blocker was consistency — fix that and two channels open at once.
  • Constraints are the brief. “It must work for self-help groups” eliminated most of the conventional answers and produced a better one.
  • Packaging is product. For a first-time buyer, it is the product — it’s the entire experience until the jar is open.
  • Next time, test the pack before defending the strategy. I’d put three packaging routes in front of real shoppers early, because a strategy that depends on a design assumption should be validated as a design assumption.
