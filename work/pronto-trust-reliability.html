Pronto: making “house help in minutes” true every time

Pronto has already won the speed race — 500,000+ homes, 10,000+ trained Pros, 11 Indian cities, a Pro at your door in fifteen minutes. So I didn’t write a PRD for a missing feature. I wrote one for the five specific moments where the promise quietly breaks, and for the realisation that most of Pronto’s support tickets aren’t support problems at all.

The product

Pronto is an on-demand house-help platform founded in 2024 and headquartered in Gurgaon. You open the app, tap once, and a trained, background-checked professional arrives in fifteen minutes — for cleaning, laundry, dishes, ironing or vegetable prep. Bookings can be instant, scheduled, or recurring, and pricing is flat and shown up front: no per-bedroom multipliers, no deposits.

It has worked. The company reports 500,000+ homes served and 10,000+ Pros across 11 cities, and has raised roughly $13M — a $2M seed from Bain Capital Ventures, then an $11M Series A co-led by General Catalyst and Glade Brook Capital. It competes with Snabbit and the far larger Urban Company.

The problem isn’t a missing feature

The instinct with a product like this is to look for the next feature. That instinct is wrong here. Pronto’s core promise — trusted house help, in minutes — is real and differentiated. What’s failing is the gap between what’s promised and what’s reliably, verifiably delivered, and it fails at five specific, recurring moments. Each one damages the brand in a different way:

The five moments where trust breaks, and what each one costs
The moment What happens What it costs the brand
The clock stops being believable Arrivals of 40–50 minutes reported against a 15-minute promise. The ETA stops being something you can plan around — the exact pain Pronto exists to remove.
The visit is a black box No live visibility unless you pay for the Verified tier. Reviewers report directing Pros task by task, and work marked done that wasn’t. The customer supervises the work themselves — re-introducing the effort they paid to eliminate.
Recovery costs more than the failure Refunds processed inconsistently, with no visible status once requested. Losing 40 minutes to recover ₹200 teaches that failures are expensive to fix. That lesson outlives the refund.
“Pronto” doesn’t mean one thing Reliability varies sharply by city; complaints cluster in newer ones. The brand becomes a lottery based on where you live, and the national average hides the failing cities.
Worker safety is structurally unsolved Pros are protected by an SOS button and self-defence training. Both only help after something has already started going wrong — the category’s central unresolved risk.

The insight that reframed the whole proposal

Reading through public complaints, a pattern emerged that changed what I thought I was writing. Customers were contacting support to do things the app simply wouldn’t let them do: cancel inside a grace window, check whether a refund had been approved, find out how long the queue was, avoid re-explaining everything to a second agent.

The reframe

Most support contacts aren’t support problems. They’re product gaps in disguise. Adding headcount treats the symptom. The fix is to make the majority of these contacts unnecessary — and to make the remainder resolve on the first touch.

That single sentence reorganised the document. It turned “improve support” — an operations budget line — into a product requirement with a measurable target, and it made the support-contact rate per booking the honest scoreboard for whether any of this worked.

How I built the evidence

I had no internal data, so I said so in the document rather than writing around it. Everything in the PRD is grounded in what’s publicly verifiable, triangulated across three sources:

  1. The live product. The marketed promise, booking types, pricing model, the paid “Pronto Verified” recorded-session add-on, and the stated support window of roughly 7 AM to 10 PM — against booking sold as 24/7.
  2. App store reviews and press coverage. Where the specific failure modes came from: the 40–50 minute arrivals, the repeated no-shows, the ~40-minute support silence followed by a second agent with no context, agents saying a cancellation “isn’t possible” before negotiating a workaround, and refunds processed inconsistently with no visible status.
  3. Sector reporting. Reuters’ April 2026 coverage of India’s on-demand house-help sector frames worker safety as the industry’s central unresolved risk, quoting an investor in a rival platform calling safety the deciding factor in long-term consumer loyalty.

What happened while I was writing this

In May 2026, Pronto faced public backlash over an AI-assisted in-home recording pilot used to train robotics systems — opt-in, and by the company’s account 0.1% of customers, but enough that India’s IT ministry took notice and both Snabbit and Urban Company publicly distanced themselves. It sharpened one of my design choices: this PRD deliberately specifies lightweight timestamped proof of completion for recurring visits rather than extending video recording, because in this market the cheapest trust signal that carries no consent burden is worth more than the richest one that does.

The proposal

In one line: make the promise Pronto already makes actually true every time, by closing the gap between what’s quoted and what’s visible, verifiable, recoverable, and enforced — on both sides of the marketplace.

Three value propositions carry it:

  • Customers can trust the clock. The ETA you see is the ETA you get — and if it slips, compensation is automatic, not something you file a complaint to receive.
  • Customers can see the visit, and fix it themselves when it breaks. Live status and task progress on every booking, plus self-serve cancellation, refund status and issue reporting that never require reaching a human.
  • Pros are protected proactively, not reactively. Safety tooling checks in on the worker during a visit, instead of waiting for her to signal that something has already gone wrong.

The conceptual model

I organised the whole document around the customer’s timeline rather than around features, so every requirement had an obvious owner and an obvious moment:

Before a visit

  • Live, honest ETA
  • Home context and preferences carried into every booking
  • Identity verified for new customers and addresses

During a visit

  • Live status and task progress visible to the customer
  • Silent safety check-ins to the Pro
  • City-level quality signals feed local ops

After a visit

  • Proof of what was completed
  • Preferences and feedback carried into the next visit

When it breaks

  • Self-serve fix first; human support second, with context and authority to act
  • Auto-resolution for objectively verifiable failures

The requirements

Thirty-two requirements across six journeys plus cross-cutting telemetry. Every one is written so it can be verified as done or not done, without prescribing design or implementation. P0 means required to call this shipped; P1 means important for a genuinely trustworthy experience; P2 is a valuable follow-on.

Selected requirements below; the full set sits in the source document.

Journey A — Booking and waiting for a Pro

  • P0 Arrival estimate derived from live conditions rather than a fixed marketed number, with notification whenever it changes materially.
  • P0 If a Pro arrives later than the estimate shown, compensation applies automatically — the customer never has to ask.
  • P1 Self-serve cancel and reschedule at any point policy allows, showing the exact refund amount before confirming.
  • P1 Bookings cancelled almost immediately, before dispatch, are refunded in full automatically.

Journey B — During the visit

  • P0 Every booking — not only the paid Verified tier — shows live status and progress against the specific tasks booked.
  • P0 The Pro sees the full task list and home notes before arriving, so the customer isn’t directing work task by task.
  • P0 The Pro receives a discreet, periodic safety check-in that doesn’t require reaching for help — distinct from, and additional to, the SOS button.
  • P1 Higher-risk visits (new customer, new address, isolated locality, late hour) are visible to local supervisors for a proactive check.

Journey C — When something goes wrong: self-serve first

  • P0 Structured issue reporting from the booking itself — no-show, late arrival, incomplete task, misconduct, unauthorised cash request, damage — not just free-text chat.
  • P0 Issues verifiable against visit data resolve automatically, refund or replacement issued with no agent review.
  • P0 Refund status and expected completion visible at any time, without asking anyone.
  • P0 A Pro with a substantiated report of soliciting unauthorised cash is suspended pending review — an enforced consequence, not an FAQ.

Journey D — When something goes wrong: reaching a human

  • P0 Live support reachable during every hour bookings can be placed. If Pronto sells a 24/7 booking, a live booking gets 24/7 help.
  • P0 Active-booking issues route ahead of post-visit issues — someone waiting in their home isn’t queued behind yesterday’s billing question.
  • P0 A second agent sees the full prior conversation and booking context. The customer never re-explains.
  • P0 Frontline agents can action refunds, cancellations and replacements within published policy without escalating — nobody is told an entitled outcome “isn’t possible.”

Journeys E & F — Coming back, and local operations

  • P0 Home context and preferences stored once and carried into every subsequent booking, visible to the assigned Pro.
  • P0 Any Pro reassignment happens inside the system and is reflected in-app. Off-platform coordination is not a supported path.
  • P1 Recurring bookings get a lightweight ongoing trust signal — timestamped proof of completion — without the cost or consent overhead of a recorded session every time.
  • P1 City ops teams see their own on-time, no-show and support-contact rates — not the company average — so a struggling city is caught internally before it surfaces publicly.

How I’d know it worked

Three outcomes, each chosen because it’s hard to game and maps directly to one of the value props:

Outcome Metric Direction Why this one
Fewer bookings need a human at all Support-contact rate per booking ↓ down Directly tests the reframe. If contacts don’t fall, the product gaps weren’t the cause.
Failures stop being expensive Median time from failure to resolved or refunded ↓ down The cost of a bad booking is the chase, not the money. This measures the chase.
Trust-related churn falls Repeat-booking rate, failed first booking vs successful first booking ↑ converge The one I’d defend the work on. It asks whether recovery actually saves a customer — not whether the queue moved faster.

The third is deliberately the hardest to move and the hardest to fake. A support team can cut handle time without a single customer coming back.

Scope discipline

A PRD is as much about what it refuses as what it asks for. This one explicitly excludes new service categories, pricing and unit-economics changes, new-city expansion, a B2B offering, and replacing Pronto Verified. Where those intersect — pacing a new city launch against the size of its certified Pro pool, for instance — the requirement is written as a constraint rather than a plan.

I also named the assumptions the document would collapse without:

  • Visit telemetry — Pro location, job start/stop, task completion — exists or can be captured, and is trustworthy enough to settle a refund with no human review. Auto-resolution is worthless if this isn’t true.
  • The published cancellation policy is what Pronto intends to honour, so the gap is enforcement rather than policy.
  • Extending live support to match booking hours is a cost the business will carry — or booking hours narrow to match support.

The questions I couldn’t answer from outside

These are in the document because pretending to know them would have been the least honest thing I could do:

Question What it changes Blocks
What share of support contacts are cancellations, refund checks and no-shows? How much load Journey C actually removes — and therefore how this work is sized. The first number I’d ask for. Sizing
Why are local ops reassigning Pros manually today? If auto-assignment is insufficient it’s an algorithm problem. If it’s habit and trust it’s a change-management problem. The fix differs sharply. Journey F
Does auto-compensation for late arrival create a gameable incentive? Whether the automatic payout needs an abuse threshold, and where it sits. Journey A
Who owns substantiation for misconduct reports? What evidence standard triggers a suspension. A firm consequence needs a defensible process behind it. Journey C
Is timestamped photo proof legally equivalent to video? Whether the lightweight trust signal clears the same in-home consent bar that governs Verified. Journey E

What writing this taught me

  • The support queue is a product spec nobody wrote. Every recurring ticket describes a job the product refused to let someone do. Reading complaints as unmet requirements rather than as noise was the most useful move I made.
  • Two-sided products need two-sided requirements. It would have been easy to write a customer-trust document. But the Pros are the supply, and in this category worker safety is the thing investors and press both name as decisive. A trust PRD that only protects the buyer isn’t a trust PRD.
  • An average hides the thing you need to see. A healthy national on-time rate and a failing city look identical on one dashboard. Requiring city-level cuts wasn’t a reporting nicety — it was the difference between catching a problem internally and reading about it.
  • Write the constraint, not the plan. For everything out of scope, stating it as a constraint kept the document from sprawling while still acknowledging the dependency.
  • What I’d do differently: I’d sequence this into shippable slices. As written it reads as one initiative, and thirty-one requirements is a programme, not a sprint. With internal data I’d cut a first release around the two P0s with the clearest payoff — honest ETAs and self-serve cancellation with visible refund status — and prove the support-contact rate moves before committing to the rest.
