BlockBloom: shipping a working DApp from first principles

I didn’t build this to become a blockchain engineer. I built it because I kept hearing “put it on chain” proposed as a solution and had no way to judge whether it was a good one. The fastest route to an opinion worth holding was to build the thing end to end.

The context

BlockBloom was a build-led programme run by the Google Developer Group at IIT Kanpur: start at distributed ledgers, finish with a deployed decentralized application. No abstractions handed to you, no framework doing the interesting part for you.

The structure was the appeal. Most of what I’d read about blockchain was either promotional or dismissive, and neither is much use when you have to decide whether a feature should be built on it. Deploying a contract yourself resolves the argument quickly, because the chain does not care what you believe about it.

What I built, in order

  1. Fundamentals. Distributed ledgers, decentralization and consensus — working through how Bitcoin actually operates, because the design decisions in everything after it are reactions to that original set of trade-offs.
  2. The Ethereum ecosystem. What smart contracts changed, and what the transition to proof-of-stake meant for cost, throughput and the security model.
  3. Tokens. Wrote and deployed token contracts in Solidity — the first point where you feel that every line of state you write has a price attached to it.
  4. NFTs. Created unique tokens with metadata, which is where the gap between “what the contract guarantees” and “what people assume it guarantees” gets uncomfortably wide.
  5. The DApp. Designed, developed and deployed a functional decentralized application, integrating the contracts behind an interface a person could actually use.

The trade-offs you only feel by building

Four things landed differently once I’d shipped rather than read about them. All four are product constraints wearing engineering clothes:

  • Every write has a price tag. Gas costs make storage a budgeting exercise. On a conventional backend, “log this too” is free; on chain it’s a per-user cost you either absorb or pass on. It changes what features are even sensible.
  • You cannot hot-fix immutability. Deployed is deployed. That inverts the usual startup rhythm — specification quality matters more than iteration speed, which is exactly backwards from everything else I’ve built.
  • Onboarding is the funnel. Wallet setup, seed phrases, gas, network selection — four opportunities to lose a user before they reach any value. Most DApps don’t have a product problem, they have a first-run problem.
  • Irreversibility is a UX property. No password reset, no chargeback, no support ticket that fixes a mistake. Designing for users who cannot be rescued is a genuinely different discipline.

The conclusion I came to

Decentralization earns its cost when you need verifiable ownership, censorship resistance, or the removal of a trusted intermediary that is genuinely a problem. Everywhere else, a database wins on every axis that matters — and being able to tell the two cases apart quickly is the actual value of having built one.

Why this matters for product work

I’m not applying for engineering roles. The reason this project sits in a product portfolio is that it changed three things about how I work with engineers:

  • I can cost a request. Not precisely — but well enough to know when “can we just also store X” is trivial and when it’s a redesign.
  • I know what a real technical objection sounds like. Which means I can tell it apart from reluctance, and take it seriously when it is one.
  • I’m harder to sell to. Having deployed the thing, I ask what a technology actually removes from the system rather than what it adds to the pitch.

What I’d do differently

  • Design the first-run experience before the contract. I built the capability then wrapped an interface around it. The interesting problem — getting a non-crypto user to their first successful transaction — deserved to be the starting point, not the finishing touch.
  • Write the threat model down. I reasoned about failure cases informally. With immutable code, informal isn’t good enough; the discipline of writing “here is how this gets abused” belongs before deployment.
  • Test the honest version of the pitch. The strongest thing I could have added is a written comparison against the boring centralised alternative — if the decentralized version doesn’t win that comparison on its own terms, it shouldn’t ship.
