Sizing the bet: finding product-market fit for a digital-first FMCG launch

A B2C company wanted to grow in two very different categories at once — nutraceuticals and digital platforms — aimed at the same urban, digital-native consumer. My job was to work out which segments were worth building for, and to find out whether the product actually landed before anyone spent money rolling it out.

Role
Market Research Analyst
Organisation
Finlatics
Timeline
May – July 2025
Focus
Segmentation, TAM/SAM, PMF validation, GTM

The context

The company sold to urban, digital-first consumers — people who research on a phone, buy on a marketplace, and abandon a product the moment it stops being convenient. It was looking at two expansion paths that, on the surface, had nothing in common: health & wellness FMCG (nutraceuticals, supplements) and tech / digital platforms.

Two categories, one customer. That’s a tempting story to tell in a pitch deck and a dangerous one to build on, because the same person buys a protein supplement and a subscription app for completely different reasons, on completely different timelines, with completely different tolerance for being wrong.

3 Independent signals used to validate fit

The question I was actually answering

The brief arrived as “research these markets.” That isn’t a question you can finish. So the first thing I did was rewrite it into three that you can:

  • Which segments are worth building for? Not “who could buy this” — who has a need sharp enough that they’d change their behaviour.
  • How big is the opportunity, honestly? A TAM number that survives being questioned by someone who wants to say no.
  • How will we know the product fits before we commit to rollout? A test we agree to in advance, so the result can’t be reinterpreted after the fact.

The principle

Research that doesn’t change a decision is a very expensive form of reassurance. Every workstream below was scoped backwards from a decision someone had to make.

Approach

  1. Segment on behaviour, not demographics. “Urban, 22–35” is a media-buying category, not a segment. I split both markets by the job the consumer was hiring the product for, how they discovered it, and what they were willing to pay for — which cuts the same age bracket into groups that behave nothing alike.
  2. Map the competitive landscape. For each candidate segment: who already serves it, what they’re good at, and where the gap is. Critically — whether the gap exists because it’s hard to serve, or because nobody has bothered yet. Only the second kind is an opportunity.
  3. Size it. Quantified TAM and SAM for the surviving segments, so “this segment looks interesting” became “this segment is worth x, and here is the arithmetic.”
  4. Test for fit against three signals. Ran structured market tests measuring engagement, redemption and NPS — chosen deliberately to disagree with each other (more on this below).
  5. Convert findings into a go-to-market call. Which segment to lead with, which channel, what the sequencing looks like, and what would have to be true for the plan to be wrong.

Why those three metrics

This was the part I’d defend hardest. Any single validation metric can be made to look good, so I picked three that fail in different directions:

  • Engagement answers are they using it? It’s the earliest signal and the easiest to fool yourself with — novelty and a launch push both look identical to genuine pull for the first two weeks.
  • Redemption answers will they act? It converts stated interest into a costly action. Someone claiming they’d buy is cheap; someone redeeming an offer has spent attention and made a small commitment.
  • NPS answers will they carry it for us? It’s the only one of the three that speaks to whether growth compounds without paid acquisition — which for a nutraceutical brand competing on shelf space is the whole game.

High engagement with weak redemption means you’ve built a habit nobody values. Strong redemption with poor NPS means you’ve bought a transaction, not a customer. Reading them together is what turns a dashboard into a decision.

What I actually did

  • Built segmentation models for health & wellness (FMCG) and tech markets, profiling urban and digitally-native consumer groups by need, discovery path and price sensitivity.
  • Designed and ran market tests using engagement, redemption and NPS to validate product fit and inform the rollout plan — with pass conditions agreed before the tests ran.
  • Evaluated the competitive landscape across both categories, identifying where incumbents were structurally weak versus merely inattentive.
  • Quantified TAM and SAM for the priority segments, carrying the assumptions explicitly so anyone could challenge them.
  • Recommended data-driven go-to-market strategies for digital platforms and nutraceutical products, sequenced by confidence rather than by size of prize.
  • Packaged consumer insights and growth recommendations into material that fed KPI definitions, moat arguments and investor-facing narratives for the B2C portfolio.

Outcome

The work landed as a prioritised set of segments with sizing attached, a validated read on which product propositions had genuine pull, and a go-to-market recommendation with an explicit order of operations. The consumer insights and growth recommendations went on to support KPI definitions, competitive-moat arguments, and investor pitch material for the B2C products.

The more durable outcome, from my side, was the framing: the team stopped asking “is this market big?” and started asking “which slice of it will move first, and what would prove us wrong?”

What I’d do differently

  • Instrument before launching, not alongside. Some of the cleanest read on early behaviour was only available in hindsight. Defining the event taxonomy up front costs a day and saves a fortnight of reconstruction.
  • Report by cohort, not in aggregate. Blended numbers hide the exact thing you’re looking for — the segment that behaves differently. I’d cut every metric by acquisition cohort from day one.
  • Pair every quantitative finding with five conversations. The numbers told me what reliably and why only by inference. Structured qualitative interviews alongside the tests would have shortened the distance to a real explanation.
  • Write the losing case. I’d now attach a short “why this recommendation fails” memo to any GTM call — it’s the fastest way to find out whether a plan is a conviction or a preference.
